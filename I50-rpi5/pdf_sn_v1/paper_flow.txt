We present what are, to our knowledge, the first 3D numerical-relativity evolutions of wormhole
binaries: pairs of massive Ellis–Bronnikov drainholes held open by a ghost scalar field. Each throat
is an unstable fixed point whose e-fold time matches the González–Guzmán–Sarbach linear rate to
within 1.9%. Two throats obey a charge-sign rule: like scalar charges repel and opposite charges
attract, with a measured attraction-to-repulsion ratio of 1.462 ± 0.022 against the predicted 3/2, so
only opposite-signed pairs merge. A head-on collision forms a common MOTS around both stillopen throats, born with about their combined area. The horizon then loses 28% of its area as it
swallows the phantom field, and by t = 100 lies within 1.4% of the Schwarzschild radius of the pair’s
mass, still shrinking. Fly-by pairs form no horizon and both mouths inflate, the one nearest capture
radiating the most gravitational-wave energy measured. A lone collapsing throat sheds its seeded
quadrupole as a burst ringing at the Schwarzschild frequency of the black hole it becomes, and every
encounter radiates its own burst of gravitational waves and a scalar dipole of comparable energy
and negative sign. A throat survives at most a few orbital periods for any plausible perturbation,
and the negative-energy dipole would pump rather than drain the orbit, so these binaries have no
inspiral.

I.

INTRODUCTION

Wheeler’s spacetime foam is a sea of handles—
wormholes that open and close on the Planck scale [1–
3]. Several mechanisms promote them to macroscopic
size: inflation stretches a Planck-scale Lorentzian wormhole with the background [4]; bubble and domainwall cosmologies produce post-inflationary regions joined
through wormholes [5, 6, 80, 81]; and a gas of relic
wormholes has been studied as a cosmology in its own
right [7, 8]. The bubble and wall wormholes are not
traversable and pinch off into black holes [6, 80]; none
of this work evolves a traversable relic—what one does,
and what two of them do to each other. That is the
subject of this paper.
Two classical facts govern the dynamics.
First,
the simplest evolvable traversable wormhole—the Ellis–
Bronnikov drainhole held open by a ghost scalar [9–
12]—is unstable [18, 55, 76]: depending on the sign
of the perturbation, the throat collapses to a black
hole or inflates, on a timescale set by the throat radius [13, 17, 64, 75, 77, 79], as 3+1 evolutions of the massless throat confirm nonlinearly [44]. As a quantum field
coupled to gravity a ghost destabilises the vacuum unless
its effective theory is cut off at low energy with Lorentz
violation [62]; we treat it as a classical phenomenological
model. Second, Geroch’s theorem forbids classical topology change [19, 20]: two wormholes cannot fuse into one,
and none can be created classically. Creation is quantum [21], so our evolutions start at t = 0 from the newborn state.

Together these facts set selection rules. No astrophysical assembly channel exists: a throat decays faster than
dynamical friction or radiation reaction could pair it.
A primordial pair born from foam needs no assembly—
birth and collision coincide, at a separation comparable to the objects—and its evolution is a race between
orbital collision and throat decay, both rates fixed by
the classical solution. The scalar adds a second rule: a
phantom field repels like charges and attracts opposite
ones, so like-signed pairs scatter while opposite-signed
pairs fall together; and the sign of a lone throat’s perturbation selects collapse—a wormhole-to-primordial-blackhole channel beside the standard ones [22–24]—or inflation. A newborn population thus splits into four fates—
merger, scattering, collapse, inflation—plus a fast plunge
to a merged core whose end state the grid does not resolve, across birth separation, perturbation and relative
sign, the space mapped here (Fig. 1). The binary evolved
has three asymptotic ends: two independent wormholes,
each leading to its own far universe. A foam handle
differs—its two mouths belong to one wormhole and carry
opposite scalar charge by construction—so our oppositesigned pairs reproduce a handle’s charges but not its
topology. What merges is not two wormholes into one:
both throats end behind one common horizon.
The observable is not a chirp but a population of bursts
from the merger epoch, redshifted into whatever band
the mass scale selects [25–28]. No throat survives more
than milliseconds to days (Sec. IV G), so neither LIGO
nor LISA [96–98] can see a wormhole in residence: a population born in the early universe converts at birth, its
bursts redshifted far below both bands, and a drainhole
signal in them would need throats born that late, which
no known mechanism supplies. The epoch-independent
deliverable is the energy radiated per event and its spec-

A.

NUMERICAL SETUP
Evolution scheme

We evolve CCZ4 [37] with damping (κ1 , κ2 , κ3 ) =
(3, 0, 1), 1+ log slicing [38] and a Gamma-driver shift [39,
40, 66] with η = 1, both started from the initial data’s
lapse and shift, fourth-order stencils, RK4 and Kreiss–
Oliger dissipation σ = 0.1. The time step is ∆t =
0.02 ∆x, an order of magnitude below the usual CCZ4
Courant factor, because larger steps fail: a multiplier of
0.1 dies at t = 16. Every resolution statement below is at
fixed ∆t/∆x; Appendix A tests convergence in resolution
and in extraction radius.

D.

Marginally outer trapped surfaces

A marginally outer trapped surface (MOTS) is a closed
two-surface of the slice on which the outgoing null expansion vanishes, θ+ = 0, while the ingoing one is negative,
θ− < 0; the outermost MOTS is the apparent horizon. A
MOTS is read off a single slice, unlike the event horizon,
and since the phantom violates the null energy condition
it need not lie inside one: every “horizon” and “censored”
in this paper is a statement about MOTSs on the evolved
slices, and “black hole” names the region inside such a
dynamical apparent horizon, whose area this matter can
shrink (Sec. IV E), not a stationary black hole, which the
remnants only approach (Sec. VI B).
The finder runs in post-processing on every archived
slice, so horizon coverage matches the plot cadence. It expands the surface about the tracked centre as r = h(θ, ϕ)
in real spherical harmonics up to degree ℓmax = 6 on
the head-on slices and ℓmax = 8 on the orbital ones,
and solves θ+ [h] = 0 by Newton iteration on metric and
extrinsic-curvature data interpolated from level 3 on the
head-on and level 2 on the orbits, whatever the run’s
finest level, with the areal orientation of Sec. III C. The
first surface of a run is found by the damped fast flow [65];
each later slice warm-starts from the previous surface
and converges in a few iterations until every projected
harmonic of θ+ is below the solver tolerance of 10−6 .

A.

THE SINGLE WORMHOLE

The static throat as a fixed point

Evolved without a declared perturbation, the exact
drainhole holds R⋆ = 3.8895 to 0.1% for 35 time units at
level 3 and to 1% until t ≃ 44 (Fig. 3(a)); at level 4 it is
exact to four digits at t = 30. The departure moves later
by 5.6–8.7 units per halving of the cell—a truncationnoise seed of fixed growth rate handed a smaller amplitude. Level 2 dies of an origin failure at t = 24.2 with
the throat still exact to 0.12%. The hold scales with the
mass: at level 3 the m = 0.5 throat holds 0.1% only to
t ≈ 17, against 35 at m = 1, and dies at its origin at
t = 26.3.

B.

The unstable mode

The departure is a single exponential mode
(Fig. 3(a,b)): fits below 4% deviation give τ = 5.12
(level 3) and 5.23 (level 4), i.e. T ≡ τ αth /R⋆ = 0.76
and 0.77, where αth = eu(rt ) = 0.575 is the static lapse
at the throat, so that T is the proper-time e-fold in
units of the throat’s light-crossing time. Our drainhole
is the γ1 = m/a = 0.5 member of the static family of
González, Guzmán and Sarbach (their metric with b = a,
γ0 = −πγ1 /2), for which their linear analysis gives
T = 0.758; their Table I spans T = 0.846 at the massless
end to 0.590 [18]. Solving the linearised field equations
of this solution ourselves—their regularised master
equation and, independently, the reduction in the gauge
δϕ = 0—gives T = 0.7584, i.e. τlin = 5.13 M . The measured e-fold matches it at both resolutions, to 0.2% at
level 3 and 1.9% at level 4; larger departures grow more
slowly. The seed is truncation error, and its sign flips
with resolution—level 3 collapses, level 4 inflates—the
two branches found in spherically symmetric evolutions
of the massless [13, 14] and massive [15, 17] throat, and
in 3D for the massless one [44], here reached from exact
massive data in 3D.

C.

Branching: collapse versus inflation

A declared kick, Eq. (10), overrides the truncation seed
(Fig. 3(c)). At ε = ±10−2 and ±10−3 the fate is always opposite to the push, each ± pair crosses at the
ε-independent t× ≃ 13.0, and a tenfold smaller kick arrives 11–12 units later at matched horizon radius—linear
response on the same mode. The branching survives refinement: at level 4, whose own seed inflates the unkicked
throat, +10−2 still forms a horizon at t = 11 and −10−2
still inflates, the twins crossing at t = 12.99 against level
3’s 13.01. The branch point is the throat’s, not the grid’s
or the kick’s. The kick also sets the clock: horizons form
at t = 11 (ε = +10−2 ) and t = 25 (+10−3 ), against t ≤ 61
for the unkicked throat (its first scanned slice after departure already holds a MOTS). At ε = ±0.1 the data are
no longer perturbative (Fig. 3(c)): +0.1 turns the minimal surface into a maximum between two minima and is
trapped from t = 1, while −0.1, which narrows the throat
to Rmin = 3.15, re-expands to 3.36 by t = 4 and then collapses as well, trapped from t = 6—against the sign rule.
Both die at the compactified origin (t = 14.1 and 15.2,
NaN in h11 ), the failure that also ends the +10−3 arm at
t = 40.1, before the lapse has collapsed anywhere. Their

box norm and 8.6–10.3 × 16π|ρ| on the shell; the usable

D.

The throat in motion

A uniformly moving throat keeps the resting throat’s
fate. The exact-boost p = 0.45 throat (Sec. II E; level 3,

where it falls 1% below its start (the resting level-3 throat:
t ≃ 44), and then collapses on the resting throat’s own
mode: τ = 5.0–6.1 against 5.12 at rest, on the same
branch—level 3 collapses—and it dies at the same numerical endpoint (t = 44.67, Fig. 3(d)). The instability
is the throat’s, carried with the motion: time dilation
(γ = 1.10) predicts 5.6 in coordinate time, which the fitted 5.0–6.1 cannot separate from 5.12; the branch and,
within the fit, the proper-time rate are unchanged.

H.

Type-I criticality

In the language of critical collapse the static throat is
consistent with a type-I critical solution [82]: a static solution with a single unstable mode (Sec. IV B), sitting
on the threshold between two end states—collapse and
inflation, where the standard problem has collapse and
dispersal. The measurements above are consistent with
both type-I signatures, though two amplitudes do not
establish the scaling. The first is time scaling: data a
distance ε from threshold stay near the critical solution
for a time τ ln(1/ε) set by its one e-fold, which is the
lifetime law of Table V, tBH ≃ 11 M + τ ln(10−2 /ε), and
both branches leave along the same mode, each ±ε pair
crossing at the same t× (Sec. IV C). The second is a mass
gap: black-hole formation turns on at finite mass, as at
the static Bartnik–McKinnon solution of Yang–Mills collapse [83], not at the vanishing mass of type-II scaling.
The ε = +10−2 and +10−3 horizons form at R = 3.88
and 3.81, both close to the throat’s own R⋆ : a tenfold
smaller kick leaves the black hole’s mass at formation almost unchanged, where type-II scaling would shrink it
as a power of ε. Shinkai and Hayward found evidence,
in spherical evolutions of the massless throat, for critical
solutions with a definite black-hole mass [13]; here the
critical solution is the static throat itself, its one unstable mode measured in 3+1 dimensions.

V.

TWO THROATS AT REST
A.

The sign rule

Two identical throats released from rest push apart;
reversing one throat’s scalar, σ = +1 → −1 in Eq. (5),
turns the push into a pull (Fig. 5(a,b)). Both forces share
one radial law, so their ratio Q is separation-independent:
no gap, mass or approach speed lets a like-signed pair fall
together, and σ = −1 is the only gravity-driven merger
channel. The flip is not a tuning: ϕ → −ϕ leaves Tab ,
and with it the one-body geometry, unchanged. The measured pull-to-push ratio at a = 2 is 1.462±0.022 (slice-toslice scatter over 15 slices only) against the point-charge,
large-separation (Q + 1)/(Q − 1) = 3/2 of Eq. (5). The
throat mass is equal in all arms, but λ (0.906 against
0.931) and MADM (2.27 against 1.56; Table II) differ between them, a systematic outside that error.

B.

The force law

How the push falls with distance is read off a ladder
(Fig. 5(c)). Four like-signed pairs, identical but for the
gap d = 12/14/16/18, are released from rest, and by
a common t = 11.5 their separations have grown by
δd = 0.4791/0.3716/0.2963/0.2406. For displacements
this small, δd at a fixed time is proportional to the
force, so under the inverse-square law of Eq. (5) δd d2
would be the same on every rung. It is not: it rises
monotonically, 69.0 → 78.0, across the ladder, which excludes inverse-square in the coordinate separation. Instead F ∝ (d + δ)−2 with δ ≈ 2.2–2.8 describes it: δ
fitted on d = 12–16 alone predicts 0.242 at d = 18,
where inverse-square gives 0.213 and the measurement
is 0.2406; the full-ladder fit gives δ = 2.65. Since δ
is a sizable fraction of the mouth radius R⋆ = 3.89,
the force is inverse-square in an effective separation exceeding the coordinate one by most of a throat radius.
Across widths, at d = 12, the push grows at every
rung (Fig. 5(d)): δd = 0.1623/0.3250/0.4791/0.7583 by
t = 11.5 for a = 1/1.5/2/3, a power law δd ∝ an with
n = 1.2–1.4 when fitted at t = 8–11.5, where point
charges, whose net push (Q−1) m2 /d2 is a2 /d2 by Eq. (5),
give n = 2.

C.

The repulsion branch

A like-signed pair keeps receding, and its throats end
as a lone throat squeezed inward does: both inflate

A.

Merger or fly-by

Varying the tangential momentum p at d = 12

at t ≈ 48, and recedes to 2.62 by t = 67.6. Both reach
their stop times with no NaN—the p = 0.45 arm with

no wall of its own—and the finder, run through each
pass, sees no trapped surface on either. The slowest arm,
p = 0.12, reaches contact (Sec. VII B), and the two faster
arms plunge: p = 0.60 reaches contact at t ≈ 40 (pit
separation 1.86 at its first segment’s stop) and p = 0.90

just past its t = 40 segment (2.4 and still falling), each
collapsing to a merged core on which no common MOTS
converges (Sec. VII D). The plunge boundary therefore
sits between p = 0.45 and 0.60: 90–120% of the circular
momentum under the combined point-charge pull, 1 + Q
times gravity’s (Eq. (5)), bracketing the circular value
itself. That pull is central: a pair that misses contact
keeps its angular momentum and swings past. But from
p = 0.25 up the approach deepens with speed—closest
approach falls from 2.32 through 2.18 to contact, against
the Newtonian expectation of a pericentre growing with
J = pd—so capture here is contact, not an innermost
orbit. Nor do the fly-by pairs escape: both start below
the circular momentum and remain bound in Newtonian
terms, and what follows a pass is set by the throats, which
inflate (Sec. VII B).
The recession after the turn is not that of two compact
throats: on both fly-bys the mouths inflate, and the constraint norm crosses the campaign’s 2.5 × 10−2 criterion
as they do, at t = 63.3 (p = 0.25) and 67.6 (p = 0.45)—
the trust windows past which nothing is quoted, from the
runs or their waves (Sec. VIII D).

At d = 12 the finder holds no common horizon at
any momentum. The slowest tangential arm, p = 0.12,

The design point that wins the race is d = 6 with a

D.

Interior failure after horizon formation

Two failure modes stand between a merged core and
its settled remnant, and each has a measured cure. The
first is a blow-up of the metric component h11 at the

merged core’s centre: the head-on’s level-5 segment at
t = 38.845, the d = 6 chain’s restarts at t = 27.5–30.5,
the p = 0.60 plunge at t = 55.5—in every case with the
global constraint norms clean to the last step. It is not
under-resolution: on the d = 6 chain the level-6 restart
dies at t = 29.4 and the level-7 restart dies earlier, at
t = 27.5 with max |K| still climbing—a finer grid tracks
the core’s collapse further and meets the failure sooner—
and raising the dissipation to σKO = 1.0 does not cure
it. What cures it is the χ floor: with min χ capped at
10−4 the 1/χ steepness runaway cannot complete, and
both the d = 6 chain and the p = 0.60 plunge cross their
death points and run to t = 100, the d = 6 final segment
then running floor-free. The second mode is the K wall:
max |K| itself running away at the merging core, 93 →
172 in one step on the p = 0.90 arm at t = 45.3. On the
d = 6 chain level 6 cleared the runaway that stopped level
5; on the p = 0.90 arm a level-5 continuation removed the
runaway but died at the same instant (t = 45.28).
Where a horizon forms, the failure is censored. The
core fails only after a common MOTS encloses it: the
d = 6 cell NaNs arrive 15–18 units after its horizon, the
head-on’s 21 after its, and lone collapses die behind their
horizons at level 3 too (ε = +10−3 at t = 40.1, 15 units
after its horizon forms; ε = +10−2 dressed with ε2 =
10−2 at t = 27.6, 14 after), while no level-4 lone collapse
dies. The failure is local, the exterior is already set, and
the finder’s surface rides through every crossing unmoved:
whatever diverges is the coordinate representation of a
merged interior, behind the horizon.
The record thus tests weak cosmic censorship outside
the physically reasonable matter it is posed for [84, 85]:
in every collapse resolved here—the head-on across its
segments, the d = 6 chain at levels 5–7, and the
lone collapses—the evolution fails, if at all, only inside
a MOTS. This is evidence for censorship under nullenergy violation, not a proof—the failure is a coordinate blow-up read on the slice, not a located curvature
singularity—and the plunges whose horizon never converges (Sec. VII A) leave their failures uncensored on the
slice, at the edge of what the grid resolves.

C.

Merger burst

The d = 6 merger’s (2, 2) burst is launched at contact—
the common MOTS of t = 13 (Sec. VII C)—peaks at

carries the record, clean, to t = 100 on the final segment

D.

Fly-by and plunge bursts

Each orbital arm emits one (2, 2) burst at its closest pass or plunge, with no ringdown on the fly-bys—
no remnant forms. Through the outermost sphere that
covers each burst (R = 28; the plunge’s, R = 20),
EGW /M = 3.53×10−2 at p = 0.25, 7.6×10−2 at p = 0.45
(damped run; the undamped control covers only R ≲ 34
to t = 70, Sec. III B), and at least 4.1 × 10−2 at p = 0.60,
each in its own pair’s measured mass (Sec. II E). Of the
measured energies the largest is at the capture boundary
(Sec. VII A), where the pass is deepest and slowest: the
p = 0.45 arch carries 2.2× the shallower fly-by’s energy
and 1.9× the plunge’s floor. Each fly-by’s arch is integrated to its run’s trust window (t − R ≤ 63.3 and 67.6);
the next sphere in reads 3.63 × 10−2 and 7.8 × 10−2 . The
plunge’s burst is cut by its run’s trust window, 12 units
past its R = 20 peak, so its energy is a floor and the

boundary-to-plunge ratio a ceiling.

E.

Vacuum twin

The chirping reference—a vacuum binary at d = 12,
p = 0.12, equal masses, no scalar—behaves as a binary black hole must: it merges, climbs the point-mass
chirp 299 → 473 Hz and turns toward the equal-mass
Kerr mode (Sec. IX A). It also calibrates the wavefront
method: its flat-topped merger envelope defeats peak
timing, which reads 0.57c on it against the 0.82c of its
whole-waveform lag, so every speed here but the plunge’s
is a correlated lag.

G.

Horizon censorship of the scalar channel

The head-on’s chain carries the scalar stream end to
end, and its horizon switches the channel off (Fig. 12 in
Appendix B). After the common MOTS closes (t = 18),
the mouths’ remaining hair leaves as a single ℓ = 1 pulse
along the collision axis, cresting later at each sphere in
causal order (t = 34 and 38 at R = 14 and 18), and the envelope then decays: log-linear fits over t = 30–80 give τ =

22/38/73 at R = 10/14/18. Underneath, the remnant
rings down its hair: fitted from t = 40 as one damped
oscillation, the dipole at R = 10 rings at ω = 0.123
and decays at 0.037, against 0.1226 and 0.0409 for the
ℓ = 1 scalar quasinormal mode of a Schwarzschild hole
of the final MOTS mass (M ω = 0.2929 − 0.0977 i [71]):
the frequency agrees to 1% for fits started anywhere over
t = 35–45, and the damping is 10% slower. Integrated
from where each sphere’s flux turns outgoing (t = 26 at
R = 10; before it the open mouths’ static hair drains
canonically inward) to t = 80, the energies keep the sign
of Sec. VIII F, Eϕ = −0.0500/ − 0.0571/ − 0.0594 in units
of m.
The d = 6 merger, the one other encounter with a horizon (common MOTS from t = 13; Sec. VII), sheds the
same way on its own glued record: its dipole crests at
t = 38 on R = 20 and falls exponentially, τ = 22 (fit over
t = 45–95), 19× down at R = 14 by the record’s end.
Without a horizon there is nothing to switch the channel off: the fly-by radiates its arch as the mouths swing
past one another, and what ends that emission is the encounter itself—the mouths leave intact, still carrying the
charges that source the dipole.
The lone throat shows the same contrast. Re-run at
level 4 with the scalar stream, the ε = +10−2 throat
collapses on schedule—a permanent MOTS from t = 11,
R falling 3.88 → 2.33 at its floor—and its scalar envelope, after the burst, falls across the spheres by t = 80,
with late e-fold times of 6–7. The unkicked level-4 throat,
which inflates with no trapped surface by scan or flow
finder, instead grows quasi-exponentially in the ℓ = 0
monopole (e-fold ≈4.6, shortening across t = 45–75), as
in a box-doubled (L = 128) copy, to 3% over t = 50–80,
so the growth is the throat’s and not the boundary’s or
the sponge’s; beyond t ≈ 80 the constraint norms grow,
and only t ≲ 80 is counted. The contrast is confounded
with the fate: the horizonless arms are also those whose
mouths inflate toward the spheres. What is measured is
that the scalar channel shuts off behind a horizon and
grows while a throat inflates.

IX.

OBSERVATIONAL SIGNATURES
A.

The frequency track

Morphology alone does not mark a drainhole burst: cut
to the signal’s duration and scaled to 60–300 M⊙ , binaryblack-hole templates (IMRPhenomD [94, 95]) match the
bursts in the aLIGO design noise above 20 Hz [96] with
fitting factors of 0.893–0.995, as they match the vacuum
control (0.935–0.951), on truncated near-zone records.
The frequency track separates a drainhole burst from a
binary black hole by what comes before the ringdown
(Fig. 9(c)). The vacuum twin peaks at f M = 0.065 and
climbs the point-mass track before turning toward the
equal-mass Kerr mode at f M = 0.0885 [70, 71]: a monotone rise, then a fixed ringdown frequency. No drain-

18
hole channel follows it. The head-on’s frequency falls, by
×0.58, from contact onto the ℓ = 2 quasinormal mode of
a Schwarzschild hole of its final MOTS mass, 397 Hz at
this calibration, which it reaches to 3% at the track’s minimum, before a late upturn: the remnant does not spin,
and the shrinking of its horizon would raise that mode,
not lower it. The fly-by’s frequency falls as the pair separates; the merger’s rises after contact; the lone collapse
rings at its own Schwarzschild mode (Sec. VIII A), 0.56
of the equal-mass Kerr one. Wherever a remnant’s ringdown is resolved—the lone throat’s, the head-on’s and
the head-on’s scalar dipole (Sec. VIII G)—it is the ordinary quasinormal spectrum of the black hole left behind,
as in any catalogue event [32]; the wormhole is in the
burst before it.

B.

Energy per event

The detector-independent deliverable is the energy per
event in units of the source’s ADM mass M , in the dominant multipole about each source’s axis (change from
the next sphere in, in parentheses): fly-by 7.6 × 10−2 M
(1.9%), merger 4.2×10−3 M (1.1%), head-on 8.5×10−3 M
(1.4%), collapsing throat 2.5 × 10−5 M (3.6%), vacuum
twin 2.4 × 10−3 M (10%). Since ΩGW scales as event
density times energy per event, these convert any bound
on ΩGW [28, 29] into a bound on the abundance of primordial drainhole encounters, provided the ghost scalar
exists at that epoch, as they do the rate-density limits of
the LVK O3 all-sky burst search [73]. The scalar channel
adds comparable energy of opposite sign (Sec. VIII F).

X.

DISCUSSION

All four fates of Sec. I occur, and the fast plunge, each
with a measured selection rule: the relative scalar sign
separates scattering from merger (Sec. V), the seed’s sign
separates collapse from inflation (Sec. IV), and the orbital momentum separates plunge from fly-by at 90–120%
of circular (Sec. VII). For detection, three results combine: no inspiral exists (Sec. X A), the wormhole phase
is a burst with no chirp ahead of it, its frequency falling or
drifting until the remnant’s ordinary ringdown takes over
(Sec. IX A), and the scalar counterpart decays once a horizon forms (Sec. VIII G). A surviving wormhole signature
is a drifting-frequency burst with a scalar counterpart; a
settled ringdown without one is already the descendant.

A.

No wormhole inspiral

A drainhole binary cannot radiate an inspiral. Each
throat’s unstable mode gives it a lifetime t ≈ τ ln(1/ε),
with τ = 5.1–5.2 in isolation. An inspiral needs many
orbits. Under the combined pull of Eq. (5), six times
gravity at large separation and softened as (d+δ)−2 at the

separations evolved (Sec. V B), a circular orbit at d = 12
has period P ≈ 75–93. No pair starts quiet enough to
wait that long: the companion itself plants the seed—the
effective inward kick of 0.4% at d = 12 (Sec. VII B)—
an ε worth t ≈ τ ln(1/ε) ≈ 28–29 units—under half a
period. Even ε = 10−15 buys only 1.9–2.4 periods, and
each further factor of ten adds only τ ln 10 ≈ 12 units.
The orbit’s demand grows with separation as P ∝ d3/2 ,
while the throat’s endurance grows only as ln(1/ε), and
the companion’s kick is part of the binary itself.
Radiation reaction cannot close the gap either: the
leading radiative moment, the scalar dipole, carries negative energy comparable to the gravitational channel’s
(Sec. VIII F), which, if the ADM balance holds, pumps
the orbit rather than draining it. The encounters evolved
here are not secular: each is settled within one passage
by the conservative pull and contact (Sec. VII A), with
both channels evolved self-consistently, and over the flyby the scalar carries only 0.56 of the gravitational energy (Sec. VIII F), so a close pass loses energy on net.
Pumping concerns the slow, wide orbits an inspiral needs,
where the dipole, the lower multipole, dominates—orbits
no throat survives to reach.
The wormhole-specific signal is therefore a short transient: within a few tens of M each mouth collapses or
inflates, and the plunge, burst and conversion of Secs. VI–
VIII are all that this source class emits as wormholes; any
long chirp belongs to the objects the throats become. The
argument holds while the throat’s unstable mode exists,
for the minimally coupled ghost scalar without spin of
the González–Guzmán–Sarbach analysis. Rotation can
remove that mode—in five dimensions above a critical
angular momentum [49], in four as perturbative analyses
indicate [50–52], with spinning Ellis wormholes existing
up to an extremal limit [48]—and other supports are radially stable [53, 54], so a spinning or differently supported
pair that outlives an inspiral is not excluded here. The
same lifetimes rule out a static ghost-supported throat
as a long-lived black-hole mimicker of the kind ringdown
and echo tests target [87, 88].

B.

The inflating half of the population

Collapse is half the census. The branch is chosen by the
perturbation’s sign (Sec. IV C), so a sign-symmetric seed
distribution converts about half of a population of lone
throats and sets the other half inflating. A companion
tilts the census toward inflation: its squeeze starts both
mouths on the inflation branch (Sec. VII B), and only
pairs whose common horizon wins the race convert—here
the head-on and the d = 6 merger, no d = 12 pair. An
inflating throat creates, from regular data, an expanding
anti-trapped region (Sec. IV F), which under the null energy condition would need an initial singularity [59, 60];
unlike the false-vacuum bubbles of those references and
the domain-wall wormholes of Ref. [6], whose inflating
interiors pinch off behind a horizon, it lies on our side of

19
the throat and grows into the surrounding space, as the
massless throat’s explosion does [13]. Over its record the
neck accumulates 1.3 areal e-folds and the boundary 3.0,
against the ∼60 of cosmic inflation [61]: the record shows
the mechanism, not the magnitude.

C.

Topological censorship

Topological censorship forbids an observer at infinity from probing the topology of a globally hyperbolic,
asymptotically flat spacetime that satisfies the null energy condition [89]. A traversable wormhole evades it
only by violating that condition, and the two branches
end the evasion in opposite ways. On the collapse branch,
alone or in a merger, the throats end inside a MOTS
while the phantom outside is swallowed or radiated away
(Secs. IV E, VI B and VIII G): the wormholes are not
removed—no classical evolution changes the number of
asymptotic ends [19]—but end hidden behind a horizon,
as the theorem would have them. On the inflation branch
no trapped surface forms and the throat stays open
(Sec. IV F): the evasion persists, and a distant observer
meets the expanding anti-trapped region of Sec. X B—
ghost field, whose energy density is nowhere positive—
with no horizon in front of it over the whole record. The
far universes enter only as compactified punctures; fluxes
through the throats into them are not measured.

D.

Scope

The results hold for one matter model and one family
of data: a single minimally coupled ghost scalar, equal
masses, one throat compactness (m/a = 1/2) and no
spin, on constraint-solved, matched initial data whose
momentum is the exact boost (Secs. II D and II E). Horizons here are MOTSs on the evolved slices, which for
matter violating the null energy condition need not lie
inside event horizons (Sec. III D). Within this family the
instability itself excludes survival from any early formation epoch to late times, whatever the tuning; a long-lived
relic requires changing the object—spin, which perturbative analyses indicate can remove the unstable mode, or
a radially stable support (Sec. X A)—and for either our
numbers no longer apply. Nonlinear evolutions of spinning throats are the natural next step.
