Binary Ellis–Bronnikov wormholes in 3+1 numerical relativity: mergers, fly-bys and
gravitational waves

We present what are, to our knowledge, the first three-dimensional numerical-relativity evolutions
of wormhole binaries: pairs of massive Ellis–Bronnikov wormholes held open by a phantom scalar
field, whose energy density is nowhere positive. We construct constraint-satisfying initial data for
single throats and for pairs, at rest and with orbital momentum, and evolve them with adaptive
mesh refinement. An isolated throat is unstable: depending on the sign of a small perturbation, it
collapses to a black hole or inflates, at the rate that linear perturbation theory predicts to within
1.9%. Two throats at rest repel when their scalar charges have the same sign and attract when the
signs are opposite, with a strength ratio close to the scalar force law’s prediction, so only oppositesigned pairs merge. A head-on collision forms a common horizon around both throats while they are
still open. The horizon then loses 28% of its area as it absorbs the scalar field, and approaches the
Schwarzschild radius of the pair’s mass. Pairs that fly past each other form no horizon, and both
throats inflate. Every encounter emits a short burst of gravitational waves and a scalar dipole wave
of comparable, negative energy. An isolated collapsing throat rings at the quasinormal frequency
of the black hole it becomes. No throat survives more than a few orbital periods, even when its
initial perturbation is tiny, so these binaries do not inspiral. The results hold for one phantom-scalar
model, equal masses, a single throat compactness and no spin.

I.

INTRODUCTION

Wheeler’s spacetime foam is a sea of handles—
wormholes that open and close on the Planck scale [1–
3]. Several mechanisms promote them to macroscopic
size: inflation stretches a Planck-scale Lorentzian wormhole with the background [4]; bubble and domainwall cosmologies produce post-inflationary regions joined
through wormholes [5, 6, 78, 79]; and a gas of relic
wormholes has been studied as a cosmology in its own
right [7, 8]. The bubble and wall wormholes are not
traversable and pinch off into black holes [6, 78]. None
of this work evolves a traversable relic. This paper asks
what one does, and what two do to each other.
Two classical facts govern the dynamics. First, the
simplest evolvable traversable wormhole is unstable [18,
56, 74]. It is the Ellis–Bronnikov wormhole held open
by a phantom scalar [9–12], which Ellis called a drainhole; we study its massive form, with nonzero Arnowitt–
Deser–Misner (ADM) mass. Depending on the sign
of the perturbation, the throat collapses to a black
hole or inflates, on a timescale set by the throat radius [13, 17, 62, 73, 75, 77]. Evolutions in 3+1 dimensions
confirm this nonlinearly for the massless throat [44]. As
a quantum field coupled to gravity a ghost destabilises
the vacuum unless its effective theory is cut off at low
energy with Lorentz violation [60]; we treat it as a classical phenomenological model. Second, Geroch’s theorem
forbids classical topology change [19, 20]: two wormholes

cannot fuse into one, and none can be created classically.
Creation is quantum [21], so our evolutions start at t = 0
from the newly created state.
These facts constrain how wormhole pairs can arise.
No astrophysical assembly channel exists, because a
throat decays faster than dynamical friction or radiation
reaction could pair it. A primordial pair born from foam
needs no assembly: it is born at a separation comparable to its throats, so its birth and its collision coincide.
Its evolution is then a race between collision and throat
decay. The scalar field sets a charge rule: a phantom
field repels like charges and attracts opposite ones, so
like-signed pairs scatter and opposite-signed pairs fall together. The instability sets a second rule: the sign of an
isolated throat’s perturbation selects inflation or collapse
to a black hole, a channel to primordial black holes beside the standard ones [22–24]. We map the resulting end
states (merger, scattering, collapse, inflation, and a fast
plunge to a merged core that the grid does not resolve)
across separation, perturbation and relative sign (Fig. 1).
Our binary has three asymptotic ends, since each of its
two wormholes leads to its own far universe. A foam handle is one wormhole whose two mouths carry opposite
charge, so our opposite-signed pairs share its charges but
not its topology. A merger does not fuse two wormholes
into one: both throats end behind one common horizon.
The observable is not a chirp but a population of bursts
from the merger epoch, redshifted into whatever band
the mass scale selects [25–28]. No throat survives more
than milliseconds to days (Sec. IV G), so neither LIGO
nor LISA [94–96] can see a surviving wormhole. A population born in the early universe collapses or inflates
soon after birth, and its bursts are redshifted far below
both bands. A drainhole signal in these bands would

supplies. The epoch-independent result is the energy radiated per event and its spectrum, in units of the source’s
ADM mass (Sec. IX B). The waveforms are extracted at
finite radius in the near zone, without extrapolation to
null infinity, and each energy carries the spread across
extraction spheres as its error (Sec. VIII).

The results hold for a single minimally coupled phantom

III.

NUMERICAL SETUP

This section gives the evolution scheme, the grid and
the diagnostics; Table IV summarises our simulations.

A.

Evolution scheme

We evolved the CCZ4 system [37] with constraintdamping parameters (κ1 , κ2 , κ3 ) = (3, 0, 1), 1+ log slicing [38] and a Gamma-driver shift [39, 40, 64] with
η = 1, both started from the initial data’s lapse and
shift. We used fourth-order stencils, RK4 time integration and Kreiss–Oliger (KO) dissipation with coefficient
σKO = 0.1. The time step ∆t = 0.02 ∆x, an order of magnitude below the usual CCZ4 Courant factor, keeps the
evolution stable. Every resolution statement below is at
fixed ∆t/∆x; Appendix A tests convergence in resolution
and in extraction radius.

B.

Grid, boundaries and regularisation

The domain is a cube of side L = 64 centred on
the system, with a 1283 base grid (∆x = 0.5). Nested
boxes, halving ∆x per level and centred on the tracked
throats (half-width 4 per throat at the finest level of the
main runs), are redrawn every 16 steps. The main runs
used three refinement levels (finest ∆x = 1/16), higherresolution runs up to level 7. Every binary with more
than three refinement levels, and two single-throat runs,
use L = 128: the same cells in a box twice as wide. Level
j thus means a finest cell ∆x = 0.5/2j . The one exception, the inflating throat (Sec. IV F), is evolved in an
octant of an L = 512 box whose 5 levels give ∆x = 1/16,
the resolution of our level-3 runs. Outer boundaries are
Sommerfeld, backed by a spherical sponge over the outer
quarter of the half-width, in which KO dissipation ramps
to σsp = 4 as the fourth power of the depth. Every
extraction sphere lies inside the sponge’s inner radius.
The sponge damps grid-scale noise but is transparent to
long-wavelength gauge and physical waves, which reflect
from the box faces (Sec. VI B). The vacuum controls
(Sec. VIII E) instead run GRTeclyn’s binary-black-hole
example with its own numerical settings and no sponge.
Each throat carries a compactified second infinity at
its centre, where χ ∼ r4 , so the evolved variables are
floored at χ ≥ 10−8 and α ≥ 10−10 . A variant (V) floors
χ at 10−20 and applies 10−8 only where 1/χ is evaluated.
Runs that differ only in the χ floor agree to four digits
after touching it. In the runs Table V marks O, the
1+ log source is off within coordinate radius 0.3 of each
tracked throat centre (the compactified far asymptotic
end) and restored by 0.8 (rt = 1.618), so that the moving
puncture does not drive the slicing. In the runs it marks
D, the scalar is damped exponentially wherever the lapse
has collapsed below 3 × 10−2 : inside merged cores, and
between fly-by mouths as they approach. The p = 0.45
fly-by, rerun without damping to t = 70, matches the
Weyl scalar Ψ4 of its damped counterpart to 2 × 10−7

D.

Marginally outer trapped surfaces

A MOTS is a closed two-surface of the slice on which
the outgoing null expansion vanishes, θ+ = 0, while the
ingoing one is negative, θ− < 0; the outermost MOTS is
the apparent horizon. Unlike the event horizon, a MOTS
is read off a single slice. Since the phantom scalar violates
the null energy condition, a MOTS need not lie inside an
event horizon. Every “horizon” and “censored” in this paper refers to MOTSs on the evolved slices. “Black hole”
names the region inside such a dynamical apparent horizon, whose area this matter can shrink (Sec. IV E), not a
stationary black hole, which the remnants only approach
(Sec. VI B).
The MOTS finder runs in post-processing on every
saved slice, so horizon coverage matches the output interval. It expands the surface about the tracked centre as r = h(θ, ϕ) in real spherical harmonics up to degree ℓmax = 6 on the head-on slices and ℓmax = 8 on
the orbital ones, and solves θ+ [h] = 0 by Newton iteration on metric and extrinsic-curvature data interpolated from level 3 on the head-on and level 2 on the
orbits, whatever the run’s finest level, with the areal
orientation of Sec. III C. The first surface of a run is
found by the damped fast flow [63]; each later slice
warm-starts from the previous surface and converges in
a few iterations, until every projected harmonic of θ+
is below 10−6 . From p

IV.

THE SINGLE WORMHOLE

This section asks how an isolated massive throat leaves
its static state, and how it ends.

A.

The static throat as a fixed point

Without an added perturbation, the exact drainhole
stays static until truncation error excites its unstable
mode. At level 3 the throat stays at R⋆ to 0.1% for
35 time units and to 1% until t ≃ 44 (Fig. 3(a)); at level
4 it is exact to four digits at t = 30. Each halving of the
cell delays the departure by 5.6–8.7 units, as expected
when a mode of fixed growth rate starts from a smaller
truncation error. Level 2 fails at the origin at t = 24.2,
before the throat departs. The static phase scales with
the mass: at level 3 the m = 0.5 throat stays within 0.1%
only to t ≈ 17, against the fiducial m = 1 throat above,
and fails at the origin at t = 26.3.

B.

The unstable mode

The departure is a single exponential mode
(Fig. 3(a,b)). Fits below 4% deviation give the efolding time τ = 5.12 (level 3) and 5.23 (level 4). In
units of the throat’s light-crossing time, the proper-time
e-folding time is T ≡ τ αth /R⋆ = 0.76 and 0.77, where
αth = eu(rt ) = 0.575 is the static lapse at the throat.
Our drainhole is the γ1 = m/a = 0.5 member of the
static family of González, Guzmán and Sarbach (their
metric with b = a, γ0 = −πγ1 /2), for which their linear
analysis gives T = 0.758 [18]. We solved the linearised
field equations of this solution ourselves, with their
regularised master equation and, independently, in the
gauge δϕ = 0; both give T = 0.7584, i.e. τlin = 5.13 M .
The measured e-folding time agrees with it to 0.2%
at level 3 and 1.9% at level 4; larger departures grow
more slowly. The initial perturbation is truncation error,
and its sign flips with resolution: level 3 collapses and
level 4 inflates. These are the two branches found in
spherically symmetric evolutions of the massless [13, 14]
and massive [15, 17] throat and in 3D for the massless
one [44]; here they are reached from exact massive data
in 3D.

C.

Branching: collapse versus inflation

An added perturbation, Eq. (10), overrides the truncation error (Fig. 3(c)). At ε = ±10−2 and ±10−3 an
outward perturbation (ε > 0) always collapses and an inward one always inflates. The radii of each ±ε pair cross
at the ε-independent t× ≃ 13.0. A tenfold smaller perturbation reaches equal horizon radius 11–12 units later,
as linear response on one mode predicts. The branching
survives refinement: at level 4, +10−2 still forms a horizon at t = 11 and −10−2 still inflates, the pair crossing at
t = 12.99 against level 3’s 13.01. The amplitude also sets
the collapse time: horizons form at t = 11 (ε = +10−2 )
and t = 25 (+10−3 ), against t ≤ 61 for the unperturbed
throat (its first output slice after the departure already
contains a MOTS). At ε = ±0.1 both runs collapse. The
+0.1 run is trapped from t = 1; the −0.1 run re-expands,
then collapses, unlike the smaller inward perturbations,
and is trapped from t = 6. Both later fail at the compactified origin with a NaN (a floating-point failure). Linear
response holds up to |ε| = 10−2 .

D.

The throat in motion

A uniformly moving throat keeps the resting throat’s
branch and rate. The Lorentz-boosted p = 0.45 throat
(Sec. II E; level 3, v = 0.41) stays at R⋆ until it falls 1%
below its start at t = 36.8, against the resting throat’s
t ≃ 44. It then collapses on the resting throat’s mode,
with τ = 5.0–6.1 against 5.12 at rest, and fails at the
same numerical endpoint (t = 44.67, Fig. 3(d)). Time
dilation (γ = 1.10) predicts τ = 5.6 in coordinate time,
which the fit cannot separate from the resting value.

E.

The collapse endpoint

The collapse branch ends in a black hole that consumes
its supporting field. On the unperturbed level-3 run the
horizon’s areal radius falls 3.23 → 2.37 over 39 units;
the ε = +10−2 run continues without failure to t = 100
behind its horizon. Ref. [44] evolved the massless throat
with the phantom’s stress-energy halved; its bounce and
horizon shedding are artifacts of the halving and do not
occur here. The pure-quadrupole run (ε2 = 10−2 , no
radial perturbation; Fig. 4) has a MOTS from t = 33,
whose radius shrinks 3.80 → 2.50 by t = 60, while |K|
grows inward and the lapse collapses to zero at the centre.
The shrinking horizon is consistent with phantom accretion [58, 72]. In spherical symmetry a massless phantom scalar can only shrink a horizon. With the doublenull metric −2e−f du dv + R2 dΩ2 , Tuv = 0 and Tvv =
−(∂v ϕ)2 , so the Misner–Sharp mass on a marginally
trapped tube obeys ∂v m = 4πR2 ef (∂v ϕ)2 ∂u R ≤ 0 and
∂u m = 0. The tube is timelike or null [57, 61], so R = 2m
can only fall. The runs perturbed with ε = +10−2 shrink
by 40% in areal radius to a floor at t ≈ 47. Their later regrowth tracks the Hamiltonian-constraint violation of the
added perturbation (Sec. II F) as it reaches the horizon;
the pure-quadrupole run, whose constraint violation has
no ℓ = 0 part, does not regrow (Fig. 4(a)). The shrinking
takes minutes even at 106 M⊙ (Table VI). Hawking’s area
law assumes the null energy condition [36, 59], which the
phantom violates.

G.

Lifetime and physical scale

A static throat survives for about τ ln(1/ε), so its lifetime grows only logarithmically as the perturbation ε
shrinks. At fixed m/a the solution is scale-free, and the
measured e-folding time in proper time is τ αth = 0.76–
0.77 R⋆ , about one light-crossing time of the throat. The
delay at equal horizon radius (Sec. IV C) puts the cost of
each decade in ε at 11–12 M ≃ τ ln 10. The unperturbed
throat’s horizon time places the level-3 truncation error
at ε ≳ 6 × 10−7 . Table VI converts these to physical
units. No static throat of astrophysical mass survives: a
stellar-mass drainhole leaves its static state within milliseconds, a supermassive one within minutes, and only
at 109 M⊙ does the lifetime reach days. The logarithm
makes this robust: a perturbation ten decades smaller
extends a 30 M⊙ throat’s lifetime only from 1.6 to 19
ms.

and dispersal. Two perturbation amplitudes show both
type-I signatures but do not establish the scaling. The
first is time scaling: data a distance ε from threshold stay
near the critical solution for a time τ ln(1/ε), which is

VII.

THE ORBITAL CHANNEL
A.

Merger or fly-by

We varied the tangential momentum p at d = 12 on
boosted, solved data (Videos 4–6, at p = 0.25, 0.60 and
0.90: youtu.be/fh3pU7xCU_I, youtu.be/-ejFKpetL1Y,
youtu.be/yzvwjC46lJY). At p = 0.25 the pair passes at
closest separation 2.32 and recedes. At p = 0.45 it passes
closer, at 2.18, and also recedes. Both fly-bys reach their
stop times with no NaN, and the MOTS finder finds no
trapped surface on either. The slowest run, p = 0.12,
reaches contact (Sec. VII B). The two fastest plunge to
a merged core on which no common MOTS converges
(Sec. VII D). The plunge boundary thus lies between 90%
and 120% of the circular momentum under the combined
pull of Eq. (5), 1 + Q times gravity’s, and so brackets the
circular value. This pull is central, so a pair that misses
contact keeps its angular momentum and swings past.
From p = 0.25 up, closest approach shrinks as p grows,
whereas a Newtonian pericentre grows with J = pd. Capture here is therefore contact, not an innermost orbit.
Both fly-by pairs start below the circular momentum and
stay bound in Newtonian terms. What follows a pass is
set by the throats, which inflate (Sec. VII B).
The recession after the turn is not that of two compact
throats. On both fly-bys the mouths inflate, and the
runs stop being reliable as they do, at t = 63.3 and 67.6
(Sec. III C); nothing later is quoted, from the runs or their
waves (Sec. VIII D).

B.

Merger against throat inflation

No d = 12 pair forms a common horizon. The slowest
run, p = 0.12, reaches contact at t ≈ 37 and inflates.
The MOTS finder, run from t = 51 until the run stops
being reliable at t = 56.5, finds no common MOTS. In a
test run with inward momentum (p = 0.25) the mouths
inflate faster than the orbit closes. Their Rmin grows by
72% by t = 24, while the infall stalls near separation 2.2,
and the finder finds no common MOTS on any output

The companion selects the branch, as an added perturbation does for an isolated throat (Sec. IV C), which
collapses when perturbed outward and inflates when perturbed inward. The companion acts on each mouth like
an inward perturbation. Each mouth starts in the isolated throat’s shape (Table II) yet grows like an isolated
throat with εeff ≈ −0.4% for d = 12 at rest and −1.2%
for d = 8. Every pair’s mouths therefore start on the
inflation branch, at any momentum, and grow on the
throat’s e-folding time τ ≈ 5.12 (Sec. IV B). A merger is
a race against this inflation. The common horizon forms
after 3.5 e-folding times for the head-on and 2.5 for the
d = 6 merger (Sec. VII C). The d = 12 approach takes 9
e-folding times to its closest pass and forms no horizon.
An off-axis merger therefore needs a short separation and
a small tangential momentum.

C.

The d = 6 merger

We therefore chose d = 6 with a small tangential momentum, p = 0.10 per mouth (about 14% of circular). A
level-3 test run merges, and the main run repeats it in
the L = 128 domain (Fig. 7). The MOTS finder finds a
common MOTS around both mouths from t = 13, while
the χ minima are still 3.6 apart. It forms with R = 5.60
and shrinks by 12.8% to R = 4.883 at t = 100, still
falling. The result is a black hole reached from an off-axis
approach. As in the head-on, the horizon forms around
both mouths at once and swallows the pair mid-inflation.
The run was restarted three times to pass the interior
failure (Sec. VII D): level 5 from t = 0 to t = 26.26;
σKO = 1.0 to t = 30.5; a χ floor (min χ = 10−4 ) to
t = 36.7; and level 4 with both settings reverted to t =
100, with no NaN. The floor acts only inside the horizon,
over t = 30–36.7. Every quoted number comes from the
finder’s surface outside it.

D.

Interior failure after horizon formation

Two failure modes appear in merged cores, and each
is removed by a change of setting. The first is a NaN in
the metric component h11 at the core’s centre, with the
global constraint norms clean to the last step. It occurs
on the level-5 head-on run, on the d = 6 run and on
the p = 0.60 plunge. Refinement does not remove it: on

the d = 6 run the level-7 restart fails earlier than the
level-6 one (t = 27.5 against 29.4), because a finer grid
follows the collapse further. Raising the dissipation to
σKO = 1.0 does not remove it either. What removes it
is the χ floor: with min χ capped at 10−4 , the runaway
of the 1/χ terms cannot complete. With the floor, both
the d = 6 run and the p = 0.60 plunge pass their failure
times and reach t = 100. The second mode is a runaway
of max |K| at the merging core, from 93 to 172 in one
step on the p = 0.90 run at t = 45.3. Level 6 removes it
on the d = 6 run. On the p = 0.90 run a level-5 restart
removes it, but that run fails at t = 45.28.
Where a horizon forms, the failure occurs inside it. The
core fails only after a common MOTS encloses it: the
d = 6 NaNs arrive 15–18 units after its horizon forms,
and the head-on’s 21 after its. Isolated throats that collapse at level 3 also fail behind their horizons (ε = +10−3 ,
15 units after; ε = +10−2 combined with ε2 = 10−2 , 14
after), and none fails at level 4. The failure is local, the
exterior is already set, and the finder’s surface passes
every failure time unmoved. What diverges is the coordinate representation of a merged interior, behind the
horizon.
Our simulations thus test weak cosmic censorship
outside the physically reasonable matter it is posed
for [82, 83]: every resolved collapse fails, if at all, only
inside a MOTS. This is evidence for censorship under
null-energy violation, not a proof, since the failure is a coordinate blow-up on the slice and not a located curvature
singularity. The plunges whose horizon never converges
(Sec. VII A) leave their failures outside any horizon on
the slice, at the edge of what the grid resolves.

mass (1.16–1.21) rather than its mass at the window’s
start (1.47).

Scalar emission after horizon formation

In the head-on run the scalar-field extraction covers the
whole evolution, and the horizon switches the channel off
(Fig. 12 in Appendix B). After the common MOTS forms
(t = 18), the mouths’ remaining scalar charge leaves as
a single ℓ = 1 pulse along the collision axis. The pulse
crests later at each sphere in causal order, and its envelope then decays. Underneath, the remnant’s scalar
dipole rings down. Fitted from t = 40 as one damped
oscillation, its frequency agrees to 1% with the ℓ = 1
scalar quasinormal mode of a Schwarzschild black hole of
the final MOTS mass [69], for fits started anywhere over
t = 35–45. Its damping is 10% slower. Integrated from
where each sphere’s flux turns outgoing to t = 80, the
energies keep the sign of Sec. VIII F.
The d = 6 merger, the only other encounter that forms
a horizon (common MOTS from t = 13; Sec. VII), loses
its scalar charge the same way over its restart segments.
Its dipole crests and then falls exponentially, with efolding time 22. Without a horizon nothing switches the
channel off. The fly-by radiates its pulse as the mouths
swing past one another, and the emission ends with the
encounter itself. The mouths leave intact, still carrying
the charges that source the dipole.
The isolated throat shows the same contrast. We
evolved the ε = +10−2 throat again at level 4 with scalarfield extraction. It collapses at the expected time, with
a permanent MOTS from t = 11, and its scalar envelope
falls across the spheres after the burst, with late e-folding
times of 6–7. The unperturbed level-4 throat inflates
with no trapped surface found by the round-sphere scan
or the MOTS finder. Its ℓ = 0 scalar monopole instead
grows quasi-exponentially, with e-folding time ≈4.6. The
same run with the domain doubled to L = 128 agrees to
3%, so the growth is the throat’s and not the boundary’s
or the sponge’s. The constraint norms grow later, so we
use only t ≲ 80. The contrast cannot be separated from
the end state: the runs without a horizon are also those
whose mouths inflate toward the spheres. What we measure is that the scalar channel shuts off behind a horizon
and grows while a throat inflates.

OBSERVATIONAL SIGNATURES
A.

The frequency track

The frequency track separates a drainhole burst from
a binary black hole by what comes before the ringdown
(Fig. 9(c)). Waveform shape alone does not: binaryblack-hole templates (IMRPhenomD [92, 93]) match the
truncated near-zone bursts in aLIGO design noise [94]
with fitting factors of 0.893–0.995, as they match the
vacuum control. The vacuum control climbs the pointmass track to a peak at f M = 0.065 and turns toward
the equal-mass Kerr mode at f M = 0.0885 [68, 69], so
its frequency rises monotonically and then stays fixed.

X.

DISCUSSION

Every end state listed in Sec. I occurs, the fast plunge
included, and each is selected by a measured rule. The
relative scalar sign separates scattering from merger
(Sec. V), the sign of the perturbation separates collapse from inflation (Sec. IV), and the orbital momentum separates plunge from fly-by at 90–120% of circular
(Sec. VII). For detection, three results matter: the binaries do not inspiral (Sec. X A), the wormhole phase is a
burst with no chirp ahead of it whose frequency falls or
drifts until the remnant’s ordinary ringdown takes over
(Sec. IX A), and the scalar counterpart decays once a horizon forms (Sec. VIII G). A wormhole signature is there-

18
fore a drifting-frequency burst with a scalar counterpart.
A ringdown without a scalar counterpart comes from the
black hole that remains.

A.

No wormhole inspiral

We find that a drainhole binary does not live long
enough to radiate an inspiral. An inspiral needs many
orbits, but each throat lives only t ≈ τ ln(1/ε), with τ =
5.1–5.2 in isolation. Under the combined pull of Eq. (5),
softened as (d+δ)−2 at the separations evolved (Sec. V B),
a circular orbit at d = 12 has period Porb ≈ 75–93. The
companion acts on each throat like an inward perturbation of 0.4% (Sec. VII B), which leaves a lifetime of 28–29
units, under half a period. Even ε = 10−15 would give
only 1.9–2.4 periods, and each further factor of ten in
ε adds only τ ln 10 ≈ 12 units. The period grows with
separation as d3/2 and the lifetime only as ln(1/ε), and
no binary is free of its companion’s perturbation.
Radiation reaction does not help either. The leading radiative moment, the scalar dipole, carries negative energy comparable to the gravitational radiation
(Sec. VIII F), which, if the ADM balance holds, pumps
the orbit rather than draining it. Pumping acts over
many orbits, whereas each encounter evolved here is settled within one passage by the conservative pull and contact (Sec. VII A). Over the fly-by the scalar radiation
carries only 0.56 of the gravitational energy (Sec. VIII F),
so a close pass loses energy on net. Pumping matters for
the slow, wide orbits an inspiral needs, where the dipole
dominates as the lower multipole, and no throat survives
to reach them.
The signal specific to wormholes is therefore a short
transient. Within a few tens of M each mouth collapses
or inflates, so the plunge, burst and collapse to a black
hole of Secs. VI–VIII are all this source emits while its
throats are open. Any long chirp belongs to the objects the throats become. The argument holds while
the throat’s unstable mode exists, as for the minimally
coupled phantom scalar without spin of the González–
Guzmán–Sarbach analysis. Rotation can remove that
mode, in five dimensions above a critical angular momentum [50] and in four as perturbative analyses indicate [51–
53]; spinning Ellis wormholes exist up to an extremal
limit [49]. Other supports are radially stable [54, 55], so
a spinning or differently supported pair that outlives an
inspiral is not excluded here. The same lifetimes rule
out a static phantom-supported throat as a long-lived
black-hole mimicker of the kind ringdown and echo tests
target [85, 86].

only by violating that condition, and the two branches
end the evasion in opposite ways. On the collapse branch,
alone or in a merger, the throats end inside a MOTS
while the phantom field outside is swallowed or radiated

C.

Scope

Our results rest on one matter model and one family of
data: a single minimally coupled phantom scalar, equal
masses, one throat compactness (m/a = 1/2), no spin,
and constraint-solved, matched initial data whose momentum is an exact boost (Secs. II D and II E). Horizons
here are MOTSs on the evolved slices, which for matter violating the null energy condition need not lie inside
event horizons (Sec. III D). Within this family the instability rules out survival from an early formation epoch
to late times. A long-lived relic needs spin or a radially
stable support (Sec. X A), both outside this family. Nonlinear evolutions of rotating throats find that rotation
slows the radial instability and, above a threshold spin,
show no evidence of it over the evolved times [46].
