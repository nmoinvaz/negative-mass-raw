Rotation slows the instability of Ellis–Bronnikov wormholes
Nikita M. Shirokov1, ∗ and Ilya Nachevsky2, †

arXiv:2610.29941v2 [gr-qc] 6 Nov 2026

1

Higher School of Economics, Moscow, Russia

Rotation weakens the radial instability of Ellis–Bronnikov wormholes in slow-rotation perturbation theory. We investigate its nonlinear effect by evolving stationary rotating drainholes supported
by a phantom scalar field. We construct the backgrounds with an independent spectral solver,
validate them against published symmetric solutions, and identify a misprint in a published field
equation that did not affect the original authors’ numerical results. Evolutions perturbed only by
discretization error show that rotation slows the collapse. At intermediate spins some throats expand, with the direction of departure depending on the effective perturbation. At the fastest evolved
spin, the throat’s areal radius shows no growing departure through evolution time t = 150 in code
units, about 28 e-folding times of the static instability, at three resolutions. Two other rapidly rotating models also show no growth over their reliable evolution intervals. Collapsing rotating throats
emit an axisymmetric quadrupolar gravitational-wave burst whose energy increases between the two
spins compared. Second-order initial data excite the instability through their background truncation
error. The results establish slower collapse and prolonged absence of detected radial growth for the
sampled asymmetric backgrounds, over finite times from initially axisymmetric data.

I.

INTRODUCTION

The Ellis–Bronnikov wormhole, a throat held open by
a phantom scalar field [1–4], is unstable: depending on
the sign of the perturbation it collapses to a black hole
or expands without bound, on a timescale set by the
throat radius [5–9]. Nonlinear 3+1 evolutions confirm
both branches for the isolated throat [10] and carry them
into binaries, in which every configuration ends in collapse, expansion or a merged horizon within a few dynamical times [11]. A persistent traversable wormhole
needs a mechanism that removes this instability.
Rotation is the oldest candidate [12]. Spinning Ellis–
Bronnikov solutions are known perturbatively [13] and,
beyond perturbation theory, from the elliptic construction of Kleihaus, Kunz and Chew [14, 15], including symmetric and asymmetric families: a sequence that starts
at the static throat and ends, at equatorial rotation velocity ve = 1, on an extremal Kerr black hole, the phantom
field vanishing in the limit; no closed form exists [16].
On the stability side, Azad, Blázquez-Salcedo, Khoo
and Kunz [17, 18] carried the radial mode to second
order in spin: the static unstable mode weakens, with
a positive O(J 2 ) correction for every asymmetry, while
for nearly symmetric wormholes a second unstable mode
grows out of a static zero mode. The illustrated mode
crossings near 30% of the extremal value concern nearly
symmetric wormholes. The authors conjecture that the
modes merge beyond the reliable range of the quadratic
approximation and the instability disappears, as in five
dimensions [20]. Quasinormal modes of the rapidly rotating family have also been computed [23], and a later

slow-rotation study derives the equations for quadrupole
perturbations [19]. In the charged analogue the merged
modes continue as a complex pair (oscillating growth),
and the growth rate reaches zero only in the extremal
limit [21]. A perturbative method cannot decide which
continuation rotation takes in four dimensions, and,
to our knowledge, no rotating wormhole has yet been
evolved nonlinearly.
This paper builds the tools for that evolution and applies them. We construct two families of backgrounds as
exact stationary solutions, distinguished by the asymmetry γ between their two ends (defined in Sec. II). The
symmetric family (γ = 0), massless when static, is the
one with published data, the curves of Ref. [14] and the
scalar charges of Ref. [15], and we use it to validate the
solver and the 3D initial data across the spin range. The
drainhole family (γ = −π), whose static member is the
mass-1 drainhole evolved in Refs. [10, 11], lies within the
asymmetric solution space studied in Ref. [15]; it is the
family we construct independently and evolve.
The solver tests every solution against identities it
never uses. These checks exposed a misprint in a published field equation (Sec. II A) and showed that secondorder initial data trigger the instability at their own
error level (Sec. V). We then put the backgrounds on
the adaptive-mesh-refinement (AMR) evolution grid, verify them at fourth order, and evolve the drainhole at
nine spins (Table IV). We find that rotation slows the
collapse, as second-order perturbation theory predicts.
Some intermediate-spin evolutions expand, with the effective perturbation determining the direction of departure. At the three sampled higher spins the throat shows
no growing areal-radius departure within the reliable intervals; the fastest evolved model remains close to its
stationary radius throughout the three-resolution study.

A misprint in the published ν equation

Refs. [14, 15] close the system with a second-order equation for ν, Eq. (12) of Ref. [15]. The printed equation
contains a misprint. Chew, Kleihaus and Kunz have confirmed that their original numerical programs used the
correct equation, so the misprint does not affect their
numerical solutions or results (personal communication).
The scalar-free component Rθθ = 0, with the second
derivatives of f and ω eliminated by Eqs. (4)–(5), gives

on all 200 saved solutions of both families to ≤ 7 × 10−8
of its largest term within 20 η0 of the throat, where the
printed form differs by up to 53%. The agreement with
their published solutions provides a separate validation
of our implementation (Sec. III).

Evolution scheme

We check the interpolated data in three steps:
1. Constraints and throat radii. At t = 0 the Hamiltonian and momentum constraint norms fall with
resolution on every refinement level (Figs. 6(d,h)
and 7(d,h) at t = 0). In radial shells on the drainhole at ve = 0.51 (L = 64, three grids), they converge at fourth order between the puncture and
the throat; farther out the tables’ interpolation
error dominates, growing as 1/∆x2 (Hamiltonian)
and 1/∆x (momentum). The throat radii along
coordinate rays converge to the tables’ values: to
1.4 × 10−4 at N = 192 on that drainhole and, on
the symmetric family at seven spins ve = 0.24–0.99
with three resolutions each, to 1 × 10−3 at worst
(N = 256).

Evolution uses GRTeclyn [28–30] with the setup of

E.

Long evolutions: the outer boundary

Evolutions that must outlive the outer-boundary crossing time t ≈ L need an outer boundary that neither
reflects nor drives the interior. Kreiss–Oliger dissipation cannot do it, since its strength falls as the sixth
power of wavelength, so the sponge layer of Refs. [10, 11]
is transparent to the long outgoing gauge pulse; and a
flat-space Sommerfeld condition assumes asymptotics the
wormhole does not have (mass 1–12 sits inside the box).
Two changes in the same outer layer fix it. The Sommerfeld condition is applied to the deviation of the evolved
fields from the stationary background rather than on the
fields themselves; and a damping layer can in addition
drive every evolved field back to that background at rate
λ, ∂t u → ∂t u − λ f (r) (u − ubg ), with f (r) the sponge’s
quartic ramp and ubg the run’s unperturbed stationary
background, rebuilt exactly from the tables; λ = 0 recovers the base scheme identically. Two otherwise identical
D21 evolutions, damping on and off, validate the layer at
λ = 1: the equatorial throat radius agrees to 2 × 10−7 ,
the near-zone Ψ4 signal to 10−3 of its peak, and the
fine-level constraint norms are unchanged, so the layer
does not touch the interior. The same comparison shows
the background-relative boundary alone keeps the coarse
outer levels cleaner, so the L = 64 collapse runs from D30
to D51 take the damping off; the D21 resolution study,
the D51 run at N = 192 and the L = 128 boxes from
D75 up run with it on (Table IV). With this boundary
the L = 64 runs, which a standard boundary limits to
the crossing time t ≈ 64, run to their final times t = 100–
150, with the converged span set by the constraint norms
instead.

Criteria, diagnostics and runs

Stability claims follow the criteria of Sec. II of Ref. [24]:
a geometric measure of the growth, for which we use
the areal radius defined below and the marginally outertrapped surface (MOTS) found spectrally on every output; constraint norms that converge at the code’s order
while the growth proceeds; an e-fold time that converges
to a nonzero value across resolutions; and its dependence
on the perturbation, tested at D21 with added perturbations of both signs (|ε| = 10−3 ; Table IV). Every run
carries three nested refinement levels on the throat, and
the domain size follows the model’s mass: MADM triples
along the family (1.112 at ve = 0.21, 3.311 at 0.70), so
the distance from the throat to the L = 64 boundary
falls from 25 MADM at slow spin to under 10 MADM at
ve = 0.70. Models up to ve = 0.51 therefore run on the
L = 64, N = 128 grid of the static evolution of Ref. [11],
and models from ve = 0.75 on L = 128, N = 256 at the
same finest ∆x. The Newman–Penrose scalar Ψ4 , whose
multipoles carry the outgoing gravitational wave, is extracted on spheres at R = 10–30 (L = 64) and R = 14–
45 (L = 128); the signal is its (ℓ, m) = (2, 0) mode.
Studies at three resolutions test convergence at the two
ends of the spin sequence (Appendix A). Table IV lists
every run, from the static reference through the O(J 2 )
regime [17, 18] to spins beyond the reach of perturbation
theory.

Measuring the growth. The diagnostic for every evolution without
p an added perturbation is the areal radius
RA (t) =
Asph (t)/(4π), where Asph is the minimum
area among the sampled coordinate spheres on a time
slice. This measures the predominantly radial departure.
The area of a specified surface is geometric, while the
choice of coordinate spheres and the evolution time retains a dependence on the spatial coordinates and slicing.
We compare this diagnostic with the circumferential radii
and independently located MOTSs.
The e-fold time τ is the inverse slope of ln |dRA /dt|
while the departure of RA from its initial value grows
from 0.2% to 1%. We fit the rate of change rather than
the departure itself because the initial transient offsets
RA by a constant that differs between resolutions, and
the derivative removes it. The choice of interval hardly
matters: τ changes by under 6% between the 0.2–1% and
0.5–2% intervals. Measured this way, τ self-converges
across the three D21 resolutions, 7.8, 6.6 and 6.1 at
N = 96, 128 and 192, at third to fourth order (Q = 2.3;
Appendix A); Richardson extrapolation at that measured
order gives the continuum value τ = 5.9. This resolution
study establishes convergence of the fitted timescale at
D21. The zero of the fit across spins below remains an

extrapolation beyond the measured collapse branch.
The collapse branch. On the spectral backgrounds the
behaviour reverses: the unperturbed throat collapses on
the static mode, later and more slowly the faster it spins.
Along the models D0–D40 the time at which the areal
radius has fallen by 1% grows steadily, and τ rises from
5.4 to 13.3 (Fig. 4; Table V). Each collapse ends in a
black hole: the D21 horizon settles at R = 2.52 (irreducible mass Mirr = R/2 = 1.26), the D30 at R = 2.567
by t = 130; the D40’s MOTS leaves the throat at t = 92
and reads R = 3.22, still shrinking, at t = 100. Each
evolution is perturbed only by its own discretization error; because τ comes from the growth rate, it does not
depend on the perturbation amplitude, which differs between runs.
The expanding branch. At ve = 0.456 (28.8% of extremal) the unperturbed throat expands instead: the
areal radius falls by 1.12% by t = 83, turns, crosses
zero at t ≈ 102 and grows exponentially, τ = 12.1. The
D51 model behaves the same way (−0.30% at t = 65–
68, then exponential growth with τ = 8.8); its companion run, differing in resolution and boundary treatment,
shrinks instead (Table V), so at this spin the direction
of departure depends on the numerical setup. The fitted
expansion rate characterizes that evolution; the direction
of departure alone does not identify a distinct unstable
mode. No marginally outer-trapped surface forms while
a throat expands, within the converged spans. Collapse
and expansion are the instability’s two end states, and
the sign of the initial perturbation selects between them.
The added radial perturbations at D21 show it directly,
as for the static throat [11]: inward expands, outward
collapses, and both depart by 1% at t ≈ 24 at the same
rate.
Rapid rotation. D90 remains close to its stationary
radius throughout the evolution: the areal drift stays

Against the perturbative prediction. Figure 4 puts

than the resolution bias (τ = 8.3 against 6.1 at ve = 0.30,

The 28.8% and 33% models have expanding evolutions,
with fitted times τ = 12.1 and 8.8. These growing departures show that those simulations have not remained
stationary. The second-mode crossings illustrated in
Ref. [18] concern C ≤ 0.2, whereas our evolutions use
C = 1. The expansion therefore does not establish that
their second mode becomes dominant or test their conjectured merger at the same parameters. Their τ belongs
to a linear mode in Killing time and ours to a fit of the
nonlinear areal radius, so the comparison at each spin is
only approximate.
Gravitational waves.
The rotating collapse radiates with no nonspherical perturbation put in (Fig. 5),
whereas the static throat radiates only once a quadrupolar perturbation is added [11]. The signal is a single (2, 0)
burst with ring-down period ≈23, propagating at the local light speed, its peak flat in extraction radius to 1.5%
over R = 30–45 on the L = 128 box, which reproduces
the L = 64 throat history to 3 × 10−4 . Aligned on each
model’s own collapse, the D30 burst has the D21’s shape
and timing at about 1.5 times the amplitude, and the
radiated energy per unit mass grows as the amplitude
squared, E20 /MADM = 3.3 × 10−7 → 8.0 × 10−7 . The
spherical static collapse radiates nothing: its measured
record at R = 14 stays below 4 × 10−9 .

VI.

CONCLUSIONS

We have constructed stationary rotating Ellis–
Bronnikov wormholes in two families from slow rotation
to ve ≈ 0.99, including the asymmetric drainhole sequence evolved here, and corrected a misprint in the published ν equation, Eq. (12) of Ref. [15], to Eq. (7). Additional field-equation checks exposed the misprint. The
original authors confirm that their numerical implementation used the correct equation and their results are
unaffected. Interpolated onto the AMR evolution grid,
the solutions reproduce the solver’s charges and throat
radii, while the O(J 2 ) backgrounds excite the instability
through their truncation error. Quantitative evolution
from these approximate backgrounds requires separate
control of that error.
The nonlinear evolutions show the collapse slowing
with spin and expanding departures in some intermediatespin simulations. The radial collapse slows from τ = 5.4
in the static model to 13.3 at 24% of the extremal angular
momentum, its rate following a − b (J/A)2 , which extrapolates to zero at 32%, beside the second-order prediction
of 33% [17, 18]. The expanding departures near 30% depend on the effective perturbation, as the two D51 setups
demonstrate. They establish growth in those simulations
without identifying a second eigenmode.
The D90 model, at 81% on this axis, shows no growing
areal-radius departure through t = 150 at three resolutions, about 28 e-folding times of the static reference.
D75 and D80 show the same absence of detected growth
over their shorter reliable intervals. These observations
provide evidence of prolonged radial stability under the
numerical perturbations present in the sampled high-spin
evolutions.
Unlike the static collapse, the rotating collapse emits a
(2, 0) gravitational-wave burst from unperturbed axisymmetric data, its energy growing with spin as the amplitude squared.
The evolutions end at t ≤ 150, so they bound growth
only over that span; a slower mode could still emerge
later. Above about 29% of the extremal angular momentum (ve ≈ 0.46) the throat’s equator lies inside an
ergoregion. Horizonless rotating objects with an ergoregion are generically subject to a slow nonaxisymmetric
instability [31, 32]; our runs, started from axisymmetric
data, bound only axisymmetric growth. Only the drainhole family (C = 1) was evolved; the symmetric family
enters through the solver validation alone. The end state
of each unperturbed run is set by the sign of its effective
perturbation, so collapse and expansion label the sign of
that perturbation, not the model. The 32% zero is an
extrapolation of the collapse-branch fit beyond the last
collapsing model at 24%, not a measured endpoint.

Appendix B: The refinement-interface mode and the
timestep

The timestep ∆t = 0.02 ∆x was adopted empirically
in Refs. [10, 11]. We traced what sets it to a grid-scale
mode pinned to the fixed coarse–fine interfaces of the
nested refinement boxes, dissipation-starved at σ = 0.1,
whose growth rate rises with the timestep. Constraint
norms evaluated per refinement level, with cells covered
by finer levels masked, expose the mode by t ≈ 10, well
before the domain-wide norm departs from its reference.
Raising the dissipation where the mode lives removes it:
at σ = 0.4 the interface-level norms sit two to five orders
of magnitude below the σ = 0.1 reference by t = 100.

The larger timesteps this would permit went untested on
the final outer-boundary setup, so all runs keep ∆t =
0.02 ∆x.
